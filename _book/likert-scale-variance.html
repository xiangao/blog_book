37  Modeling variation, not just the mean, in Likert-scale data

Published

July 16, 2026

37.1 Why bother modeling the variance of a Likert item

Most of the time, when we regress a Likert-scale outcome (a 1–7 satisfaction item, a 1–5 agreement item, a peer-evaluation rating) on some covariates, we only model the mean. But plenty of real questions are about the spread, not the average: do evaluators agree more about some employees than others? Does one group of raters show more disagreement than another? Is a training program tightening or loosening the distribution of responses, not just shifting its center?

The natural instinct is to compute an SD (or variance) for each unit — each subject’s ratings across raters, each respondent’s answers across items — and regress that on your covariates, the same way you’d regress a mean. This chapter is about why that instinct runs into a specific, avoidable problem on a bounded scale, and what to do about it instead.

37.2 The problem: on a bounded scale, variance is mechanically tied to the mean

Here is the part that is easy to miss: on a scale with a hard floor and ceiling, the maximum possible variance a set of responses can have depends on where their mean sits. This isn’t a tendency in typical data – it’s a mathematical fact, true for any distribution on a bounded interval.

For any random variable \(X\) confined to \([a,b]\), both \((X-a)\) and \((b-X)\) are non-negative for every draw, so their product is too:

\[(X-a)(b-X) \geq 0 \quad \text{for every } X \in [a,b]\]

Taking expectations preserves the inequality, and expanding the left side gives \(E[X^2] \leq (a+b)\mu - ab\) where \(\mu = E[X]\). Substituting into \(\text{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - \mu^2\):

\[\text{Var}(X) \leq (a+b)\mu - ab - \mu^2 = (\mu - a)(b - \mu)\]

This is the Bhatia–Davis inequality (Bhatia and Davis 2000). Equality is achieved, not just approached: put all the mass at the two endpoints, with \(P(X=a) = (b-\mu)/(b-a)\) and \(P(X=b) = (\mu-a)/(b-a)\), and you get variance exactly \((\mu-a)(b-\mu)\) – the most polarized a set of responses can possibly be, given that mean.

On a 1–7 Likert item, that’s

\[\text{SD}_{\max}(\mu) = \sqrt{(\mu-1)(7-\mu)}\]

a downward parabola, widest at the scale’s midpoint (\(\mu=4\), where \(\text{SD}_{\max}=3\)) and pinched to exactly zero at both ends. A respondent whose raters all pick 7 has, by definition, zero disagreement – there’s nowhere else for a rating to go.

mu_grid <- seq(1, 7, length.out = 200)
sd_max  <- sqrt(pmax((mu_grid - 1) * (7 - mu_grid), 0))

ggplot(data.frame(mu = mu_grid, sd_max = sd_max), aes(mu, sd_max)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1, color = "#2d5fa3") +
  labs(x = "mean rating (μ)", y = expression(SD[max](mu)),
       title = "Maximum possible SD, as a function of the mean") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12)

The consequence: any two groups whose means differ will have different ceilings on their SD, whether or not anything about their true dispersion differs. If most of your respondents already sit near one end of the scale (a very common situation – satisfaction items, performance ratings, and health scales are all typically skewed toward one end), this isn’t a corner case. It’s the region where the curve above is steepest, and where raw SD comparisons are most likely to mislead.

NoteA finite-sample refinement

The curve above is the population bound. For a genuinely finite sample of \(n\) real numbers with mean \(\mu\), the true maximum is strictly tighter still: Ellis (2025) gives it exactly as \(\text{Var}_{\max}(n,\mu) = (\mu-1)(7-\mu) - \tfrac{36}{n}a(1-a)\), where \(a\) is the fractional part of \(n(\mu-1)/6\), which simplifies to \((\mu-1)(7-\mu) - 9/n \leq \text{Var}_{\max}(n,\mu) \leq (\mu-1)(7-\mu)\). At \(n=5\) raters, that’s up to 1.8 points\(^2\) of extra slack – worth knowing if your groups are rated by only a handful of people each, since the population curve above then understates how tight the true ceiling is.

A natural first fix is to divide the SD (or variance) by the mean, the way you would for a coefficient of variation. This doesn’t work here, because that correction is designed for a different mechanism – variance scaling proportionally with the mean, as in count data – not a ceiling that pinches toward zero at both ends. \(\text{SD}_{\max}(\mu)/\mu\) is still a curve (zero at \(\mu=1\), a peak of \(0.75\) at \(\mu=4\), back to zero at \(\mu=7\)), just a differently-shaped one. Dividing by the mean doesn’t remove the mean-dependence; it just relabels it.

37.3 Three ways to actually model it

37.3.1 Gaussian location-scale model (MELSM)

The simplest fix that people reach for is a mixed-effects location-scale model: model the mean as usual, and also model the residual SD as a function of covariates.

library(brms)

fit_gaussian <- brm(
  bf(rating ~ group + (1 | subject),
     sigma ~ group),                 # model the SD, not just the mean
  data = df, family = gaussian()
)

brms puts a log link on sigma by default, so the group coefficient on the sigma part is a difference in log-SD; exponentiate it to get an SD ratio between groups.

The catch: a Gaussian model has no boundary in it. It doesn’t know the scale stops at 1 and 7, so it has no way to distinguish “this group’s raters genuinely agree more” from “this group’s mean happens to sit closer to the ceiling.” Both produce the identical signature in the fitted sigma.

37.3.2 Beta regression: model precision instead of variance directly

If your outcome can be rescaled to \((0,1)\) (e.g. \((y-1)/6\) for a 1–7 item), Beta regression gives you a mean/precision parameterization built exactly for bounded outcomes (Ferrari and Cribari-Neto 2004; R implementation in Cribari-Neto and Zeileis 2010’s betareg package). With mean \(\mu\) and precision \(\varphi\):

\[\text{Var}(Y) = \frac{(\mu-1)(7-\mu)}{1+\varphi} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \varphi = \frac{(\mu-1)(7-\mu)}{\text{Var}(Y)} - 1\]

Set \(\varphi = 0\) and this is the Bhatia–Davis bound – the maximum possible variance given \(\mu\). So \(\varphi\) is dispersion measured after the boundary effect is divided out, and \(\varphi \sim \text{group}\) is a comparison that doesn’t inherit the ceiling confound the way raw SD does.

The catch here is smaller but real: it’s a continuous distributional assumption grafted onto what is usually genuinely discrete, ordinal data (a 1–7 Likert response isn’t a continuous proportion), and dividing by a small-sample variance estimate can behave badly when a handful of raters happen to agree by chance.

\(\varphi\) itself is awkward to report – it lives on \((0,\infty)\) with no natural reference point. Its bounded, more interpretable twin is

\[R := \frac{\text{Var}(Y)}{\text{Var}_{\max}(\mu)} = \frac{1}{1+\varphi}\]

By Bhatia–Davis, \(\text{Var}(Y) \leq \text{Var}_{\max}(\mu)\) for any distribution on \([1,7]\) – not just Beta ones – so \(R \in [0,1]\) always, with no distributional assumption required. \(R=1\) means a subject’s raters are as polarized as the ceiling allows, given their mean; \(R=0\) means perfect consensus. It’s just \((\mu, \text{Var}(Y))\) re-expressed in mean-neutral units. We’ll come back to \(R\) in the worked example below, because it turns out to illustrate the small-sample catch very concretely.

37.3.3 Ordinal cumulative-probit MELSM: put the boundary inside the model

The cleanest fix treats the 1–7 response as what it is: a discretized slice of a latent continuous trait, cut by six ordered thresholds. brms supports this directly, and lets you model both the location and the “discrimination” (inverse latent SD) as functions of covariates (Bürkner and Vuorre 2019):

fit_ordinal <- brm(
  bf(rating ~ group + (1 | subject),
     disc ~ 0 + group),           # discrimination = 1 / latent SD
  data = df, family = cumulative("probit"), init = 0,
  prior = c(
    prior(constant(0), class = "b", coef = "groupA", dpar = "disc"),
    prior(normal(0, 1), class = "b", coef = "groupB", dpar = "disc")
  )
)

The constant(0) prior pins the reference group’s discrimination at zero (latent SD = 1); only the comparison group’s disc is estimated. Because the boundary is now an explicit part of the model – six estimated cutpoints on an unbounded latent scale – a group whose mean sits near 7 doesn’t mechanically show a tighter latent SD the way its raw SD is forced to.

37.4 Worked example: two groups, identical true dispersion, different means

To see the confound in action, simulate two groups of 100 subjects each, 6 raters per subject. Both groups’ raters draw from a latent continuous opinion with the same true SD (\(\sigma = 1\)) – there is no real disagreement difference to find. Group A’s latent mean sits high (near the ceiling once discretized); Group B’s sits in the middle of the range.

set.seed(42)

n_per_group <- 100
n_raters    <- 6
sigma_true  <- 1.0   # SAME latent SD in both groups -- no real difference
mu_A <- 2.4          # group A's latent mean sits near the ceiling
mu_B <- 0.6          # group B's sits mid-range

subjects <- data.frame(
  subject = 1:(2 * n_per_group),
  group   = rep(c("A", "B"), each = n_per_group),
  mu      = rep(c(mu_A, mu_B), each = n_per_group)
)

df <- subjects[rep(seq_len(nrow(subjects)), each = n_raters), ]
df$latent <- rnorm(nrow(df), mean = df$mu, sd = sigma_true)

# six fixed cutpoints -> seven observed categories, 1 through 7
cuts <- c(-2, -1.2, -0.5, 0.2, 1.0, 1.8)
df$rating     <- as.integer(cut(df$latent, breaks = c(-Inf, cuts, Inf), labels = 1:7))
df$rating_ord <- factor(df$rating, ordered = TRUE, levels = 1:7)

On the raw scale, Group A averages 6.64 with an SD of 0.58; Group B averages 4.89 with an SD of 1.22 – the pooled correlation between subject-level mean and SD is \(-0.74\). That pattern looks like a real, sizable disagreement gap, and the Gaussian MELSM agrees with it: the fitted sigma_groupB coefficient of 0.68 (on the log-SD scale) implies Group B’s SD is 1.97 times Group A’s (95% CI: 1.82–2.14) – a difference whose credible interval is nowhere near zero.

But there is no true difference. Both groups’ raters were drawn from a latent distribution with the same SD (\(\sigma=1\)) by construction – the only thing that differs between the groups is where their mean sits relative to the ceiling. The ordinal cumulative-probit model’s disc_groupB estimate is \(-0.015\) (95% CI: \(-0.171\) to \(0.146\)) – indistinguishable from zero, correctly recovering the fact that the two groups’ underlying dispersion is identical. The Gaussian model’s “Group B disagrees twice as much” is not a finding about the raters; it’s the mechanical ceiling effect from the previous section, expressed as a regression coefficient.

37.4.1 What about R, on this same data?

\(R\) was introduced above as mean-independent by construction – so it should land on the same answer as the ordinal model: no real group difference. Compute it per subject, using the exact finite-sample bound from the earlier callout (\(n=6\) raters):

var_max_finite <- function(n, mu) {
  c <- (mu - 1) / 6
  a <- (n * c) %% 1
  36 * (c * (1 - c) - (1 / n) * a * (1 - a))
}

subj_R <- df |>
  group_by(subject, group) |>
  summarise(mean_rating = mean(rating),
            var_pop = mean((rating - mean(rating))^2),   # population (1/n) convention
            .groups = "drop") |>
  mutate(var_max = var_max_finite(n_raters, mean_rating),
         R = ifelse(var_max > 1e-8, var_pop / var_max, NA_real_))

knitr::kable(
  subj_R |> group_by(group) |>
    summarise(n_undefined = sum(is.na(R)), mean_R = round(mean(R, na.rm = TRUE), 3)),
  caption = "Per-subject R, using each subject's own 6 ratings for both mean and variance."
)

This is worse than the raw SD comparison, not better: Group A’s per-subject \(R\) averages 0.63, Group B’s 0.20 – an even larger apparent gap, and in the opposite direction from what the ceiling artifact would predict. Fourteen Group A subjects even have an undefined \(R\) (all six raters happened to pick 7, so both the observed variance and the mechanical ceiling are simultaneously zero). Has \(R\) failed?

No – the theoretical guarantee (\(R\in[0,1]\) at every \(\mu\), no forced relationship to the mean) is about the population quantity. Plugging in a mean estimated from only 6 raters breaks it, because the finite-sample bound in the denominator is itself estimated from that same noisy mean, and it is far more sensitive near the ceiling than in the middle of the scale:

knitr::kable(
  subj_R |> group_by(group) |>
    summarise(mean_var_pop = round(mean(var_pop), 3),
              mean_var_max = round(mean(var_max, na.rm = TRUE), 3)),
  caption = "The denominator (mean_var_max) is nearly 7x smaller for Group A -- not because Group A's true ceiling is that much tighter, but because a 6-rater sample mean near 7 makes the *estimated* ceiling collapse."
)

A 0.374 0.984

A Group A subject’s 6 raters average close to 7 purely because that’s where their true mean sits – but exactly how close is noisy, and the finite-sample bound shrinks fast as the sample mean approaches the boundary. A modest, unremarkable observed variance divided by an unluckily tiny denominator produces a large \(R\), for no reason connected to real disagreement. Swapping in the simpler continuous bound (no finite-sample correction) shrinks the gap a great deal – 0.181 vs. 0.173 – but each value is still an average of 100 individually noisy single-subject estimates. Only pooling each group’s full 600 ratings into one mean and one variance before computing \(R\) removes the small-sample noise entirely:

pooled <- df |> group_by(group) |>
  summarise(mean_rating = mean(rating), var_pop = mean((rating - mean(rating))^2)) |>
  mutate(var_max_cont = (mean_rating - 1) * (7 - mean_rating),
         R_pooled = round(var_pop / var_max_cont, 3))

knitr::kable(pooled |> select(group, mean_rating, R_pooled),
             caption = "R computed once per group, from all 600 ratings pooled.")
R computed once per group, from all 600 ratings pooled.
group mean_rating R_pooled
A 6.638333 0.219
B 4.893333 0.211

Pooled, \(R\) is 0.22 for Group A and 0.21 for Group B – essentially identical, agreeing with the ordinal model’s null finding. The lesson isn’t “don’t use \(R\)” – it’s that \(R\), like \(\varphi\), is only as trustworthy as the sample size behind the mean and variance plugged into it. A hand-computed per-subject \(R\) with 6 raters can manufacture a larger spurious gap than raw SD, in the opposite direction. The ordinal model never has this problem in the first place, because it never computes a per-subject summary statistic at all – disc_groupB is estimated directly from the full, pooled 600-observation likelihood per group, with the same effect as the “pooled” calculation above but done coherently, and with a proper posterior interval instead of an average of point estimates.

37.5 Takeaways

NoteSummary
  • On any bounded scale, the maximum possible variance is a function of the mean – the Bhatia–Davis inequality, \(\text{Var}(X) \leq (\mu-a)(b-\mu)\) – not a tendency to check for, a fact to design around.
  • Dividing SD (or variance) by the mean does not remove this confound; the mechanism isn’t proportional scaling, it’s a ceiling that pinches at both ends.
  • A Gaussian location-scale model can estimate sigma ~ x, but inherits the confound, because it has no boundary in it.
  • Beta regression’s precision parameter \(\varphi\) (and its bounded twin \(R\)) remove the confound algebraically, at the cost of a continuous approximation to discrete data – and \(R\) is only as trustworthy as the sample size behind the numbers plugged into it. With few raters per subject, a hand-computed per-subject \(R\) can manufacture a larger spurious group gap than raw SD, in the opposite direction.
  • An ordinal cumulative-link mixed model puts the boundary where it belongs – as estimated thresholds on a latent scale – and is the cleanest way to ask “is this dispersion difference real?” on Likert data, because it never needs a per-unit summary statistic at all.

References – Bhatia, R., and Davis, C. (2000). “A Better Bound on the Variance.” American Mathematical Monthly 107(4): 353–357. Ellis, J. L. (2025). “The maximum variance of a finite dataset, given its mean, minimum, and maximum.” arXiv:2508.17525. Ferrari, S. L. P., and Cribari-Neto, F. (2004). “Beta Regression for Modelling Rates and Proportions.” Journal of Applied Statistics 31(7): 799–815. Cribari-Neto, F., and Zeileis, A. (2010). “Beta Regression in R.” Journal of Statistical Software 34(2): 1–24. Bürkner, P.-C., and Vuorre, M. ```

The consequence: any two groups whose *means* differ will have different
*ceilings* on their SD, whether or not anything about their true dispersion
differs. If most of your respondents already sit near one end of the scale
(a very common situation -- satisfaction items, performance ratings, and
health scales are all typically skewed toward one end), this isn't a
corner case. It's the region where the curve above is steepest, and where
raw SD comparisons are most likely to mislead.

::: {.callout-note}
## A finite-sample refinement

The curve above is the *population* bound. For a genuinely finite sample of
$n$ real numbers with mean $\mu$, the true maximum is strictly tighter still:
Ellis (2025) gives it exactly as $\text{Var}_{\max}(n,\mu) = (\mu-1)(7-\mu) -
\tfrac{36}{n}a(1-a)$, where $a$ is the fractional part of $n(\mu-1)/6$, which
simplifies to $(\mu-1)(7-\mu) - 9/n \leq \text{Var}_{\max}(n,\mu) \leq
(\mu-1)(7-\mu)$. At $n=5$ raters, that's up to 1.8 points$^2$ of extra
slack -- worth knowing if your groups are rated by only a handful of people
each, since the population curve above then understates how tight the true
ceiling is.
:::

A natural first fix is to divide the SD (or variance) by the mean, the way
you would for a coefficient of variation. This doesn't work here, because
that correction is designed for a *different* mechanism -- variance scaling
proportionally with the mean, as in count data -- not a ceiling that pinches
toward zero at *both* ends. $\text{SD}_{\max}(\mu)/\mu$ is still a curve
(zero at $\mu=1$, a peak of $0.75$ at $\mu=4$, back to zero at $\mu=7$), just
a differently-shaped one. Dividing by the mean doesn't remove the
mean-dependence; it just relabels it.

## Three ways to actually model it

### Gaussian location-scale model (MELSM)

The simplest fix that people reach for is a mixed-effects location-scale
model: model the mean as usual, and *also* model the residual SD as a
function of covariates.

```r
library(brms)

fit_gaussian <- brm(
  bf(rating ~ group + (1 | subject),
     sigma ~ group),                 # model the SD, not just the mean
  data = df, family = gaussian()
)
```

`brms` puts a log link on `sigma` by default, so the `group` coefficient on
the `sigma` part is a difference in *log*-SD; exponentiate it to get an
SD ratio between groups.

**The catch:** a Gaussian model has no boundary in it. It doesn't know the
scale stops at 1 and 7, so it has no way to distinguish "this group's raters
genuinely agree more" from "this group's mean happens to sit closer to the
ceiling." Both produce the identical signature in the fitted `sigma`.

### Beta regression: model precision instead of variance directly

If your outcome can be rescaled to $(0,1)$ (e.g. $(y-1)/6$ for a 1--7 item),
Beta regression gives you a mean/precision parameterization built exactly for
bounded outcomes (Ferrari and Cribari-Neto 2004; R implementation in
Cribari-Neto and Zeileis 2010's `betareg` package). With mean $\mu$ and
precision $\varphi$:

$$\text{Var}(Y) = \frac{(\mu-1)(7-\mu)}{1+\varphi} \qquad \Longrightarrow
\qquad \varphi = \frac{(\mu-1)(7-\mu)}{\text{Var}(Y)} - 1$$

Set $\varphi = 0$ and this *is* the Bhatia--Davis bound -- the maximum
possible variance given $\mu$. So $\varphi$ is dispersion measured *after*
the boundary effect is divided out, and $\varphi \sim \text{group}$ is a
comparison that doesn't inherit the ceiling confound the way raw SD does.

The catch here is smaller but real: it's a continuous distributional
assumption grafted onto what is usually genuinely discrete, ordinal data
(a 1--7 Likert response isn't a continuous proportion), and dividing by a
small-sample variance estimate can behave badly when a handful of raters
happen to agree by chance.

$\varphi$ itself is awkward to report -- it lives on $(0,\infty)$ with no
natural reference point. Its bounded, more interpretable twin is

$$R := \frac{\text{Var}(Y)}{\text{Var}_{\max}(\mu)} = \frac{1}{1+\varphi}$$

By Bhatia--Davis, $\text{Var}(Y) \leq \text{Var}_{\max}(\mu)$ for *any*
distribution on $[1,7]$ -- not just Beta ones -- so $R \in [0,1]$ always,
with no distributional assumption required. $R=1$ means a subject's raters
are as polarized as the ceiling allows, given their mean; $R=0$ means
perfect consensus. It's just $(\mu, \text{Var}(Y))$ re-expressed in
mean-neutral units. We'll come back to $R$ in the worked example below,
because it turns out to illustrate the small-sample catch *very* concretely.

### Ordinal cumulative-probit MELSM: put the boundary inside the model

The cleanest fix treats the 1--7 response as what it is: a discretized slice
of a latent continuous trait, cut by six ordered thresholds. `brms` supports
this directly, and lets you model *both* the location and the "discrimination"
(inverse latent SD) as functions of covariates (Bürkner and Vuorre 2019):

```r
fit_ordinal <- brm(
  bf(rating ~ group + (1 | subject),
     disc ~ 0 + group),           # discrimination = 1 / latent SD
  data = df, family = cumulative("probit"), init = 0,
  prior = c(
    prior(constant(0), class = "b", coef = "groupA", dpar = "disc"),
    prior(normal(0, 1), class = "b", coef = "groupB", dpar = "disc")
  )
)
```

The `constant(0)` prior pins the reference group's discrimination at zero
(latent SD = 1); only the comparison group's `disc` is estimated. Because the
boundary is now an explicit part of the model -- six estimated cutpoints on an
*unbounded* latent scale -- a group whose mean sits near 7 doesn't
mechanically show a tighter latent SD the way its raw SD is forced to.

## Worked example: two groups, identical true dispersion, different means

To see the confound in action, simulate two groups of 100 subjects each,
6 raters per subject. Both groups' raters draw from a **latent** continuous
opinion with the *same* true SD ($\sigma = 1$) -- there is no real
disagreement difference to find. Group A's latent mean sits high (near the
ceiling once discretized); Group B's sits in the middle of the range.

```{r}
#| label: simulate
#| echo: true
#| message: false
#| warning: false
#| cache: true

set.seed(42)

n_per_group <- 100
n_raters    <- 6
sigma_true  <- 1.0   # SAME latent SD in both groups -- no real difference
mu_A <- 2.4          # group A's latent mean sits near the ceiling
mu_B <- 0.6          # group B's sits mid-range

subjects <- data.frame(
  subject = 1:(2 * n_per_group),
  group   = rep(c("A", "B"), each = n_per_group),
  mu      = rep(c(mu_A, mu_B), each = n_per_group)
)

df <- subjects[rep(seq_len(nrow(subjects)), each = n_raters), ]
df$latent <- rnorm(nrow(df), mean = df$mu, sd = sigma_true)

# six fixed cutpoints -> seven observed categories, 1 through 7
cuts <- c(-2, -1.2, -0.5, 0.2, 1.0, 1.8)
df$rating     <- as.integer(cut(df$latent, breaks = c(-Inf, cuts, Inf), labels = 1:7))
df$rating_ord <- factor(df$rating, ordered = TRUE, levels = 1:7)
```

The raw, per-subject summary already shows exactly the pattern that tempts
people into thinking there's a real disagreement difference:

```{r}
#| label: raw-summary
#| echo: true
#| message: false
#| warning: false

subj_summary <- df |>
  group_by(subject, group) |>
  summarise(mean_rating = mean(rating), sd_rating = sd(rating), .groups = "drop")

knitr::kable(
  subj_summary |> group_by(group) |>
    summarise(mean_of_means = round(mean(mean_rating), 2),
              mean_of_sds   = round(mean(sd_rating), 2)),
  caption = "Raw per-subject summary by group -- true latent SD is IDENTICAL in both."
)
```

```{r}
#| label: scatter
#| echo: true
#| message: false
#| warning: false
#| fig-cap: "Subject-level mean vs. SD, with the theoretical ceiling overlaid."

ggplot(subj_summary, aes(mean_rating, sd_rating, color = group)) +
  geom_jitter(width = 0.05, height = 0, alpha = 0.5) +
  geom_line(data = data.frame(mean_rating = mu_grid, sd_rating = sd_max),
            aes(mean_rating, sd_rating), color = "grey40", inherit.aes = FALSE) +
  labs(x = "subject mean rating", y = "subject SD",
       title = "Group A's raw SD looks smaller -- purely because its mean sits\nnear the grey ceiling curve, not because raters agree more") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12)
```

Now fit both a Gaussian MELSM and the ordinal cumulative-probit MELSM to the
same data.

```{r}

#| echo: true

#| warning: false
#| cache: true

fit_gaussian <- brm(
  bf(rating ~ group + (1 | subject), sigma ~ group),
  data = df, family = gaussian(),
  chains = 2, iter = 2000, warmup = 1000, cores = 2, seed = 1,
  refresh = 0, silent = 2
)
```

```{r}

#| echo: true
#| message: false
#| warning: false
#| cache: true

fit_ordinal <- brm(
  bf(rating_ord ~ group + (1 | subject), disc ~ 0 + group),
  data = df, family = cumulative("probit"), init = 0,
  prior = c(
    prior(constant(0), class = "b", coef = "groupA", dpar = "disc"),
    prior(normal(0, 1), class = "b", coef = "groupB", dpar = "disc")

```

```{r}
#| label: compare-results
#| echo: true
#| message: false
#| warning: false

knitr::kable(round(fixef(fit_gaussian), 2), caption = "Gaussian MELSM fixed effects (sigma part is on the log-SD scale)")
knitr::kable(round(fixef(fit_ordinal), 2), caption = "Ordinal MELSM fixed effects (disc part: 0 = same as reference group)")
```

On the raw scale, Group A averages 6.64 with an SD of 0.58; Group B averages
4.89 with an SD of 1.22 -- the pooled correlation between subject-level mean
and SD is $-0.74$. That pattern looks like a real, sizable disagreement gap,
and the Gaussian MELSM agrees with it: the fitted `sigma_groupB` coefficient
of 0.68 (on the log-SD scale) implies Group B's SD is **1.97 times** Group
A's (95% CI: 1.82--2.14) -- a difference whose credible interval is nowhere
near zero.

But there is no true difference. Both groups' raters were drawn from a
latent distribution with the *same* SD ($\sigma=1$) by construction --
the only thing that differs between the groups is where their mean sits
relative to the ceiling. The ordinal cumulative-probit model's
`disc_groupB` estimate is $-0.015$ (95% CI: $-0.171$ to $0.146$) --
indistinguishable from zero, correctly recovering the fact that the two
groups' underlying dispersion is identical. The Gaussian model's "Group B
disagrees twice as much" is not a finding about the raters; it's the
mechanical ceiling effect from the previous section, expressed as a
regression coefficient.

### What about R, on this same data?

$R$ was introduced above as mean-independent by construction -- so it should
land on the same answer as the ordinal model: no real group difference.
Compute it per subject, using the exact finite-sample bound from the earlier
callout ($n=6$ raters):

```{r}
#| label: r-measure
#| echo: true
#| message: false
#| warning: false

var_max_finite <- function(n, mu) {
  c <- (mu - 1) / 6
  a <- (n * c) %% 1
  36 * (c * (1 - c) - (1 / n) * a * (1 - a))
}

subj_R <- df |>
  group_by(subject, group) |>
  summarise(mean_rating = mean(rating),
            var_pop = mean((rating - mean(rating))^2),   # population (1/n) convention
            .groups = "drop") |>
  mutate(var_max = var_max_finite(n_raters, mean_rating),
         R = ifelse(var_max > 1e-8, var_pop / var_max, NA_real_))

knitr::kable(
  subj_R |> group_by(group) |>
    summarise(n_undefined = sum(is.na(R)), mean_R = round(mean(R, na.rm = TRUE), 3)),
  caption = "Per-subject R, using each subject's own 6 ratings for both mean and variance."
)
```

This is worse than the raw SD comparison, not better: Group A's per-subject
$R$ averages 0.63, Group B's 0.20 -- an even larger apparent gap, and in the
opposite direction from what the ceiling artifact would predict. Fourteen
Group A subjects even have an *undefined* $R$ (all six raters happened to
pick 7, so both the observed variance and the mechanical ceiling are
simultaneously zero). Has $R$ failed?

No -- the theoretical guarantee ($R\in[0,1]$ at every $\mu$, no forced
relationship to the mean) is about the *population* quantity. Plugging in a
mean estimated from only 6 raters breaks it, because the finite-sample bound
in the denominator is itself estimated from that same noisy mean, and it is
far more sensitive near the ceiling than in the middle of the scale:

```{r}
#| label: r-diagnostic
#| echo: true
#| message: false
#| warning: false

knitr::kable(
  subj_R |> group_by(group) |>
    summarise(mean_var_pop = round(mean(var_pop), 3),
              mean_var_max = round(mean(var_max, na.rm = TRUE), 3)),
  caption = "The denominator (mean_var_max) is nearly 7x smaller for Group A -- not because Group A's true ceiling is that much tighter, but because a 6-rater sample mean near 7 makes the *estimated* ceiling collapse."
)
```

A Group A subject's 6 raters average close to 7 purely because that's where
their true mean sits -- but exactly *how* close is noisy, and the
finite-sample bound shrinks fast as the sample mean approaches the boundary.
A modest, unremarkable observed variance divided by an unluckily tiny
denominator produces a large $R$, for no reason connected to real
disagreement. Swapping in the simpler continuous bound (no finite-sample
correction) shrinks the gap a great deal -- 0.181 vs. 0.173 -- but each
value is still an average of 100 individually noisy single-subject
estimates. Only pooling each group's full 600 ratings into one mean and one
variance before computing $R$ removes the small-sample noise entirely:

```{r}
#| label: r-pooled
#| echo: true
#| message: false
#| warning: false

pooled <- df |> group_by(group) |>
  summarise(mean_rating = mean(rating), var_pop = mean((rating - mean(rating))^2)) |>
  mutate(var_max_cont = (mean_rating - 1) * (7 - mean_rating),
         R_pooled = round(var_pop / var_max_cont, 3))

knitr::kable(pooled |> select(group, mean_rating, R_pooled),
             caption = "R computed once per group, from all 600 ratings pooled.")
```

Pooled, $R$ is 0.22 for Group A and 0.21 for Group B -- essentially
identical, agreeing with the ordinal model's null finding. The lesson isn't
"don't use $R$" -- it's that $R$, like $\varphi$, is only as trustworthy as
the sample size behind the mean and variance plugged into it. A hand-computed
per-subject $R$ with 6 raters can manufacture a *larger* spurious gap than
raw SD, in the opposite direction. The ordinal model never has this problem
in the first place, because it never computes a per-subject summary
statistic at all -- `disc_groupB` is estimated directly from the full,
pooled 600-observation likelihood per group, with the same effect as the
"pooled" calculation above but done coherently, and with a proper posterior
interval instead of an average of point estimates.

## Takeaways

::: {.callout-note}
## Summary

- On any bounded scale, the *maximum possible* variance is a function of the
  mean -- the Bhatia--Davis inequality, $\text{Var}(X) \leq (\mu-a)(b-\mu)$ --
  not a tendency to check for, a fact to design around.
- Dividing SD (or variance) by the mean does not remove this confound; the
  mechanism isn't proportional scaling, it's a ceiling that pinches at both
  ends.
- A Gaussian location-scale model can estimate `sigma ~ x`, but inherits the
  confound, because it has no boundary in it.
- Beta regression's precision parameter $\varphi$ (and its bounded twin $R$)
  remove the confound algebraically, at the cost of a continuous
  approximation to discrete data -- and $R$ is only as trustworthy as the
  sample size behind the numbers plugged into it. With few raters per
  subject, a hand-computed per-subject $R$ can manufacture a *larger*
  spurious group gap than raw SD, in the opposite direction.
- An ordinal cumulative-link mixed model puts the boundary where it belongs
  -- as estimated thresholds on a latent scale -- and is the cleanest way to
  ask "is this dispersion difference real?" on Likert data, because it never
  needs a per-unit summary statistic at all.
:::
